## Textbook figure captions

### Fig. 1 · Complex I — NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase

**Figure 1.** L-shaped Complex I. NADH is oxidized at the tip of the peripheral arm (N-module). The flavin FMN accepts a two-electron hydride and dispatches electrons one at a time down an electrical wire of seven on-pathway Fe–S clusters (cluster N1a remains off-pathway) to ubiquinone (Q-module). Electron transfer triggers long-range electrostatic pulses through the membrane arm (P-module), pumping **four protons per electron pair ($2e^−$)** into the intermembrane space. In vivo, Complex I often associates with Complexes III and IV into a respirasome (supercomplex). Inhibited by **rotenone**.

### Fig. 2 · Complex II — succinate dehydrogenase

**Figure 2.** A citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) enzyme embedded directly in the inner membrane. Succinate oxidizes to fumarate at SDHA, transferring two electrons to FAD. The pair cascades through three Fe–S clusters (in SDHB) to reduce ubiquinone. Heme b is present near the membrane interface; rather than serving as the main electron conduit, it is thought to limit reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation and stabilize the complex. Because the redox potential difference between succinate and ubiquinone is near zero ($\Delta E^\circ \approx 0\text{ V}$), the energy drop cannot drive proton translocation (**0 protons pumped per electron pair**).

### Fig. 3 · Complex III — cytochrome bc₁ (Q-cycle)

**Figure 3.** The Q-cycle dimer and electron bifurcation. Quinol ($\text{QH}_2$) docks at the $\text{Q}_o$ site. Oxidation splits the electron pair: one electron travels high-potential via the mobile Rieske Fe–S protein to **cytochrome $c_1$** and onto soluble cytochrome $c$; the second electron drops low-potential through hemes $b_\text{L}$ and $b_\text{H}$ to the $\text{Q}_i$ site to partially reduce an oxidized quinone to a semiquinone. Over two turns, two electrons proceed onward to cytochrome $c$ while two matrix protons regenerate one $\text{QH}_2$. Net output: **four protons released into the intermembrane space per electron pair ($2e^−$)**. Inhibited by **antimycin A** at the $\text{Q}_i$ site.

### Fig. 4 · Complex IV — cytochrome c oxidase

**Figure 4.** Cytochrome c delivers electrons one at a time to the dinuclear $\text{Cu}_A$ site, which relays them through heme $a$ into the binuclear catalytic center ($\text{heme } a_3\text{–Cu}_B$). For every **electron pair ($2e^−$)**, two protons are pumped across the membrane and two protons are taken from the matrix to reduce $1/2\ \text{O}_2$ into one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (or scaled to a full dioxygen: $4e^− + 4\text{H}^+_\text{matrix} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$, with four protons pumped). The binuclear cage tightly binds intermediates until reduction is complete, preventing the escape of dangerous reactive oxygen species. Inhibited by **cyanide ($\text{CN}^−$)**, **carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$)**, and **azide ($\text{N}_3^−$)**.

### Fig. 5 · Complex V — ATP synthase (rotary motor)

**Figure 5.** The proton-motive turbine. Protons driven by electrochemical back-pressure flow through the membrane-embedded $\text{F}_O$ domain (written with the letter O for oligomycin-sensitive), rotating the $c$-ring and attached $\gamma$-rotor shaft at speeds up to about 100 to 150 revolutions per second. This asymmetric rotation forces conformational changes within the catalytic $\alpha_3\beta_3$ hexamer ($\text{F}_1$ head), generating 3 ATP per full $360^\circ$ turn. Synthesizing and exporting one ATP costs roughly 4 protons. Jamming $\text{F}_O$ with **oligomycin** builds proton back-pressure until electron transit across the upstream respiratory complexes slows to a dead trickle.

## Full voiceover scripts (modal audio)

### CI

Complex One: an L-shaped proton pump and the primary entry gate of the chain. N A D H docks at the peripheral arm tip, handing off a two-electron hydride. F M N takes both electrons, then dispatches them one at a time down an electrical wire of seven on-pathway iron-sulfur clusters — an eighth cluster, N-one-a, sits off-pathway — straight to a quinone waiting in the membrane. That energetic fall drives mechanical shifts that pump four protons per electron pair into the intermembrane space. In real membranes, Complex One often docks directly with Complexes Three and Four into a single respirasome supercomplex. Shut this gate with rotenone, and the chain stalls right at the start.

### CII

Complex Two: succinate dehydrogenase — the only citric acid cycle enzyme bolted straight into the membrane. Succinate is oxidized to fumarate on S D H A, passing two electrons through F A D, across three iron-sulfur clusters, and past a guardian heme b to reduce ubiquinone. Heme b plays a debated role, likely quenching dangerous electron leaks rather than carrying the main current. Notice what is missing: zero protons are pumped. The energy drop from succinate to quinone is nearly zero — simply not enough voltage to pump protons. Without Complex One's four protons, an electron pair from succinate or F A D H two yields only about one and a half A T P, compared to two and a half from N A D H. Malonate, a succinate lookalike, jams S D H A shut.

### CIII

Complex Three: cytochrome b c one — home of the Q-cycle and the master of electron bifurcation. Each quinol from the membrane pool docks at the Q-oh site and splits its electron pair along two pathways: one electron hops to the swinging Rieske iron-sulfur protein, through cytochrome c-one, and out to soluble cytochrome c; the second electron dives downward through the b hemes to the Q-eye site, recycling to rebuild another quinol using matrix protons. Per electron pair consumed, four protons are released into the intermembrane space. Lock the Q-eye site with antimycin A, and electrons jam in place, backing up the entire pool.

### CIV

Complex Four: cytochrome c oxidase — the catalytic finish line where oxygen is converted into water. Cytochrome c delivers electrons one by one into copper A, passing them through heme a to the binuclear center: heme a-three and copper B. There, four electrons, four matrix protons, and one oxygen molecule snap together into two harmless water molecules, while four protons are pumped across the membrane — that is two pumped protons per electron pair. The binuclear cage holds oxygen locked until reduction is complete, ensuring no toxic partial radicals escape. Cyanide, carbon monoxide, or azide clamp this center shut, halting aerobic respiration within seconds.

### CV

Complex Five: A T P synthase — nature's rotary generator. Protons driven by electrochemical back-pressure pour through F-oh — named with the letter O for oligomycin — spinning the c-ring and central gamma axle at speeds up to about one hundred to one hundred fifty revolutions per second. Each full rotation forces the catalytic alpha-three beta-three head through three conformations, squeezing out three fresh A T P molecules. Because synthesizing and exporting one A T P takes roughly four protons, ten protons from N A D H produce two and a half A T P, while six protons from succinate yield one and a half. Jam F-oh with oligomycin, and the proton gradient builds until extreme back-pressure slows upstream electron transport to a complete trickle.
